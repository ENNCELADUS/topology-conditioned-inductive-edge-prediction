\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Protein--protein interactions (PPIs) are the wiring diagram of the cell: they
determine signal transduction, complex assembly, and the molecular mechanisms
through which genetic variation becomes disease. Establishing that wiring
experimentally remains slow, expensive, and incomplete. The human interactome is
estimated to contain on the order of $74{,}000$--$200{,}000$ physical
interactions, yet only $3{,}988$ interactions---between $5.3\%$ and $5.8\%$ of
the curated total---are confidently shared across UniProt, BioGRID, and STRING,
so even the assembled ground truth is sparse and mutually inconsistent
\citep{zhang2025humanproteome}. The search space is quadratic and severely
imbalanced: roughly $200$ million human protein pairs are physically possible,
of which an estimated $0.325\%$--$1.5\%$ actually interact
\citep{dunham2022benchmark}. Machine learning from \emph{intrinsic} protein
features---sequence, predicted or resolved structure, and protein language model
(pLM) embeddings---is therefore the only route that scales to a full proteome,
and the field has begun to deliver at that scale: a recent proteome-wide screen
evaluated all ${\sim}200$ million human pairs and reported $17{,}849$
interactions at $90\%$ expected precision, of which $3{,}631$ were absent from
every prior experimental screen \citep{zhang2025humanproteome}. Crucially, the
scientific object of interest is not the isolated pair call but the
\emph{network} it belongs to. Functional module detection, disease-gene
prioritization, pathway reconstruction, and drug-target identification all
consume an interactome rather than a list of independently scored pairs, and a
prediction set that is individually plausible but collectively implausible
serves none of them.

The dominant formulation, however, is precisely a list of independently scored
pairs. Almost all intrinsic-feature PPI predictors---from concatenated pLM
embeddings \citep{wu2024mapeppi,szymborski2024intrepppid} to jointly encoded
paired-sequence models \citep{liu2025plminteract,ullanat2026language} to
structure- and interface-based scorers \citep{zhang2025humanproteome}---estimate
$P(\mathrm{edge} \mid x_u, x_v)$ for each queried pair in isolation. This is
cleanly inductive, but it is topology-blind, and the resulting failures are
documented from three independent directions. First, the headline accuracies of
this school are substantially artifacts of leaky evaluation: a baseline that
observes nothing but node degree recovers roughly $93.3\%$--$95.5\%$ accuracy on
standard PPI benchmarks, matching most published deep models
\citep{bernett2024cracking}, and once leakage is genuinely removed, deep
sequence-based predictors plateau near $0.65$ accuracy (best model $0.645$,
$F_1$ $0.622$) against a random-forest baseline at $0.577$
\citep{reim2025plateau}. The same benchmarks admit further confounds: because only
${\sim}31\%$ of randomly sampled multi-species pairs share an organism, a model
that has learned nothing but taxonomy already reaches ${\sim}85\%$ accuracy and
$0.85$ AUROC \citep{hallee2025taxonomists}, and a sequence model's apparent edge
over classical functional-genomic features (AUPRC $0.68$ vs.\ $0.65$) dissolves
once the test set is stratified by degree, with the same model scoring AUC $0.59$
on hub--lone pairs but $0.73$ on lone--lone pairs
\citep{lannelongue2024pitfalls}. Second, and more fundamentally, accurate
pairwise scores do not assemble into plausible networks: PRING, which reframes
PPI prediction from pairs to graphs, reports that across every baseline it tested
the highest graph similarity of the assembled prediction---edge-set Dice against
the true interactome---remains below $0.5$, despite competitive pairwise numbers
\citep{zheng2025pring}. This is not a tuning failure but a consequence of the
formulation: edge labels in a graph are coupled, and node representations
computed \emph{independently} are provably unable to distinguish structurally
distinct pairs, which is why link prediction requires target-conditioned rather
than independent representations \citep{zhang2021labeling}. Third, the graph
learning literature has established that conditioning on topology is worth a
great deal---on \texttt{ogbl-ppa}, modeling common-neighbor structure explicitly
raises Hits@100 from $18.67\%$ for a plain GCN to $61.42\%$
\citep{wang2024ncn}---but every such method reads that structure from an
\emph{observed} graph containing the query nodes. Under a strict inductive
protocol this is unavailable, and structural predictors collapse accordingly:
transferred to a setting in which both endpoints are genuinely unseen, a
state-of-the-art structural link predictor falls from $90.88\%$ to $0.01\%$
Hits@20 on \texttt{ogbl-ddi} \citep{chatterjee2023disentangling}. The field is
thus caught between two incomplete options---inductive but topology-blind
pairwise scoring, or topology-aware but transductive graph-native
prediction---and the natural repairs to the former (auxiliary graph-statistic
losses, post-hoc denoising of the assembled graph, or abandoning topology
entirely in favor of a stronger attribute-pair encoder) all leave the individual
edge decision itself unconditioned on any local structure.

We argue that inductive edge prediction should instead be modeled as
\textbf{topology-conditioned binary classification}. Given a queried pair $(u,v)$
of previously unseen proteins with frozen intrinsic features, our method
retrieves graph-free candidate neighbors for each endpoint from feature space,
generates a sparse local topology scaffold over the queried nodes and their
candidates, and predicts the binary edge label \emph{in the context of that
scaffold}. The generated topology is strictly an intermediate representation: it
is never the output, it is never scored as the task, and the model's prediction
remains a $0/1$ decision for the queried pair. Because the scaffold is
constructed only from frozen features and never from the target graph, the
protocol stays strictly inductive; because the edge classifier consumes it, the
decision can depend on plausible local degree, density, clustering, and
shared-neighborhood structure in a way that independent pairwise scoring cannot
express. Using a generative module as intermediate context for a discriminative
decision, rather than as an end in itself, has clear precedent outside
graphs---in low-shot recognition \citep{wang2018lowshot}, zero-shot feature
generation \citep{xian2018fgn}, and neighbor-feature augmentation for graph
neural networks \citep{liu2022localaug}---and we adapt it to supply
\emph{topology} rather than features. We evaluate under a node-disjoint inductive
split and report edge-level and assembled-graph metrics jointly, following the
joint-reporting discipline that both the PPI benchmark literature
\citep{zheng2025pring,lannelongue2024pitfalls} and the graph generation evaluation
literature \citep{thompson2022evaluation,obray2022evaluation} have converged on.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formalize \emph{topology-conditioned inductive edge prediction}:
    binary edge classification for unseen nodes in which locally generated
    topology is input-side context, distinguishing it from both independent
    pairwise scoring and graph generation.
    \item We characterize the \emph{pair-to-topology gap}---the empirical
    divergence between edge-level accuracy and assembled-graph realism---and show
    that it is not removable by threshold selection.
    \item We propose a local-scaffold edge classifier that retrieves candidate
    neighborhoods from frozen features, generates local topology, and conditions
    the edge decision on it without any target-graph access at inference.
    \item We evaluate against topology-blind scoring, retrieval without
    adjacency, post-hoc denoising, and graph-statistic losses under one shared
    protocol, and report edge-level and assembled-graph metrics together.
\end{enumerate}
